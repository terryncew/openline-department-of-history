## Limits and source handling

Primary-source records are inspectable through Cornell's transcriptions; they have not been independently collated against page scans in this research pass. Cornell's institutional narrative describes acquittal as involving knowledge of the locked door. The app deliberately avoids overclaiming either innocence or a particular courtroom causal theory from that short summary.

The Fourth Report is the commission's own account of its work. Legislative enactment is documented; its claims of broad historical success are not accepted as measured outcomes. Avoid saying reforms ended unsafe work or independently quantifying lives saved.

The two scholarly-source access limitations are explicit in the product data. McEvoy's article abstract and Greenwald's publisher synopsis suffice for narrow, attributed summaries of their stated arguments; neither is represented as full-text reading. Markowitz/Rosner's original essay supplies an independently inspectable historian-authored alternative emphasis.

Historical 1911–1915 texts are public domain in the United States. Cornell webpage design, later transcriptions/curation, modern scholarship, and state-hosted advocacy material are not assumed freely licensed. Retain bibliographic metadata, original brief paraphrases and URLs; do not distribute full contemporary pages or book/article content without permission. No pictures needed.

## Editorial interpretation

Comparisons are editorial questions about observation, documentation and authority. They are not additional historical facts. A first-time visitor should see that "law enacted" and "safety improved by a measured amount" are different claims. This is the key falsifiable boundary in this case.
