# OpenLine History Lab Case B — Evidence Dossier: Israel–Palestine, Civilian Protection, and Durable Arrangements

**Core Inquiry:** *What documented historical interventions have reduced civilian harm or sustained negotiated arrangements, and under what conditions did they succeed or fail?*

**Epistemic & Methodological Standard:** This dossier strictly separates (1) verified primary treaty, peacekeeping, and operational humanitarian records, (2) international judicial findings bounded by their exact procedural posture (distinguishing *prima facie* plausibility orders under ICJ Statute Article 41, non-contentious Advisory Opinions under ICJ Statute Article 65, and *ex parte* pre-trial arrest warrants under Rome Statute Article 58 from final merits judgments or criminal convictions), (3) competing historiographical and political-science frameworks, and (4) unresolved empirical and structural uncertainties. No historical settlement is treated as a universal template.

## 2. Comparative Synthesis of Historical Interventions (`comparison` & `outcomes`)

### 2.1 Diplomatic and Monitoring Arrangements: Why 1979 (Sinai/MFO) and 1994 (Jordan) Endured While 1949 (Armistice) and 1993–1995 (Oslo) Broke Down

The primary documentary record reveals a sharp structural divide between arrangements that stabilized interstate frontiers for decades and interim mechanisms that eroded into renewed warfare:

1. **The 1949 Armistice Regime & UNTSO ([Egyptian-Israeli General Armistice Agreement](https://peacemaker.un.org/en/node/9440); [UNTSO Mandate](https://untso.unmissions.org/en/mandate)):** Negotiated at Rhodes under UN Mediator Ralph Bunche, the 1949 agreements successfully halted open interstate combat from the 1948 war and created Mixed Armistice Commissions (MACs) supported by unarmed [UNTSO](https://untso.unmissions.org/en/monitoring-armistice-agreements) military observers. However, Article V(2) of the [Egyptian-Israeli General Armistice Agreement](https://peacemaker.un.org/en/node/9440) explicitly stipulated that the Armistice Demarcation Line (ADL) was a purely military line and not a political or territorial boundary. Because the agreements deferred the Palestinian refugee crisis and left sovereignty inside Demilitarized Zones (such as El Auja on the Egyptian front and the Syrian/Jordanian DMZs) ambiguous, unarmed UNTSO observers were repeatedly thrust into arbitrating political sovereignty disputes without enforcement powers, culminating in the collapse of the Egyptian armistice in 1956 and the Jordanian/Syrian armistices in June 1967.
2. **The 1979 Egypt–Israel Treaty of Peace & the Multinational Force and Observers ([MFO Origins](https://www.mfo.org/origins); [MFO Mission](https://www.mfo.org/our-mission); [Treaty of Peace PDF](https://mfo.fra1.digitaloceanspaces.com/treaty_of_peace_d00d1ccf50.pdf)):** Following the 1973 October War and the 1974–1975 Sinai Disengagement Agreements (monitored by the U.S. Sinai Field Mission), the 26 March 1979 Treaty of Peace resolved the core sovereignty dispute upfront—restoring the entire Sinai Peninsula to Egyptian sovereignty—while protecting Israeli security through Annex I's four-zone demilitarization architecture (Zones A, B, and C in Sinai; Zone D in Israel) with explicit numeric caps on troops, tanks, and artillery. When the UN Security Council deadlocked on 18 May 1981 over deploying a UN force, the parties signed the [Protocol of 3 August 1981](https://www.mfo.org/origins) creating the independent MFO outside the UN framework. Three institutional features sustained this arrangement for over 45 years:
   - **Tripartite Financial and Political Ownership:** Equal one-third funding by Egypt, Israel, and the United States gave both treaty parties direct institutional ownership alongside sustained U.S. military and economic inducements ([Carnegie Endowment 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/02/gaza-trump-board-of-peace-negotiations-israel-lessons-from-oslo)).
   - **High-Frequency Verification and Daily Liaison:** Under Annex I, the MFO conducts bi-monthly verifications across all zones, responds to party verification requests within 48 hours, monitors freedom of navigation through the Strait of Tiran, and convenes daily working-level meetings between the Egyptian Liaison Agency with International Organizations (LAWIO) and the Israel Defense Forces International Cooperation Division (IDFICD) ([MFO Our Mission](https://www.mfo.org/our-mission)).
   - **Consensual Treaty Adaptability:** Rather than breaking when security conditions shifted in northern Sinai and along the Gaza border, the MFO's liaison system enabled formal agreed amendments (1 September 2005, 11 July 2007, 30 April 2018, and 7 November 2021) adjusting Egyptian Border Guard deployments in Zone C with full Israeli consent ([MFO Our Mission](https://www.mfo.org/our-mission)).
3. **The 1994 Israel–Jordan Treaty of Peace ([Wadi Araba Treaty](https://peacemaker.un.org/en/node/9431); [UNTS Record](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/showDetails.aspx?objid=080000028009bc5b&clang=_en)):** Signed on 26 October 1994 between two sovereign states that had maintained tacit security understandings for decades, the treaty finalized the international boundary (Article 3 and Annex I), institutionalized transboundary water sharing via the Joint Water Committee (Article 6 and Annex II), and recognized Jordan's historic custodianship role regarding Muslim Holy shrines in Jerusalem (Article 9). Backed by U.S. debt relief and security guarantees ([Carnegie Endowment 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/02/gaza-trump-board-of-peace-negotiations-israel-lessons-from-oslo)), the treaty has endured for over 32 years, even as escalations in the West Bank, Jerusalem, and Gaza kept societal relations in a state of "cold peace."
4. **The 1993–1995 Oslo Accords ([Oslo I Declaration of Principles](https://peacemaker.un.org/en/node/9432); [Oslo II Interim Agreement](https://peacemaker.un.org/en/node/9434)):** In contrast to the Egyptian and Jordanian treaties, Oslo I (Article V) and Oslo II (Chapter 2, Articles XI–XIII) established a five-year interim autonomy arrangement between an occupying state and a non-state national liberation movement (the PLO) without defining the final territorial end-state. By partitioning the West Bank into fragmented islands of Palestinian civil/security control (Areas A and B) surrounded by Area C (roughly 60% of the West Bank under full Israeli civil and military control), omitting an explicit textual freeze on Israeli settlement construction, and relying on bilateral joint committees without third-party enforcement or sanctions, the interim framework left both populations exposed to spoilers. Following the 4 November 1995 assassination of Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin by an Israeli extremist, a wave of suicide bombings by Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad, rapid settlement expansion under anti-Oslo Israeli coalitions, and the collapse of the July 2000 Camp David summit, the interim arrangement fractured into the Second Intifada ([Carnegie Endowment 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/02/gaza-trump-board-of-peace-negotiations-israel-lessons-from-oslo)).

## 3. International Legal Findings & Allegations: Strict Procedural Status (`facts` & `limits`)

A core requirement of historical-legal verification is preventing the conflation of distinct judicial procedures, evidentiary thresholds, and jurisdictional scopes:

1. **ICJ Contentious Proceedings — *South Africa v. Israel* (Genocide Convention, Case 192):**
   - **Procedural Posture:** Incidental proceedings for Provisional Measures under **Article 41 of the ICJ Statute** ([ICJ Case 192 Orders](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/192/orders)).
   - **Evidentiary Standard:** At the provisional measures stage, the Court does **not** make a final determination on whether the State of Israel has violated the Genocide Convention. Instead, the Court determines whether it has *prima facie* jurisdiction under Article IX of the Convention and whether the rights asserted by the Applicant are **plausible** ([Order of 26 January 2024 Summary PDF](https://www.un.org/unispal/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/192-20240126-sum-01-00-en.pdf), para. 54: *"at least some of the rights claimed by South Africa and for which it is seeking protection are plausible"*) and face a real and imminent risk of irreparable prejudice before a final merits judgment.
   - **Binding Legal Effect of the Three 2024 Orders:** Under settled ICJ jurisprudence (*LaGrand*), provisional measures orders under Article 41 create binding international legal obligations. Across its Orders of **26 January 2024** (para. 86), **28 March 2024**, and **24 May 2024** ([Summary of Order of 24 May 2024](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/summary-of-icjs-order-24may24/), para. 57), the Court ordered Israel to: (i) take all measures within its power to prevent acts falling within Article II of the Genocide Convention; (ii) ensure its military does not commit such acts; (iii) prevent and punish direct and public incitement to genocide; (iv) take immediate and effective measures to enable the unhindered provision at scale of urgently needed basic services and humanitarian assistance (including keeping the Rafah crossing open); (v) **immediately halt its military offensive, and any other action in the Rafah Governorate, which may inflict on the Palestinian group in Gaza conditions of life that could bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part** (24 May 2024 Order, para. 57(2)(a)); and (vi) preserve evidence and submit compliance reports. In all three Orders, the Court also expressed grave concern over the hostages abducted on 7 October 2023 and called for their immediate and unconditional release. Merits proceedings remain ongoing, with the Court's Order of 21 May 2026 fixing time-limits for the Reply and Rejoinder ([ICJ Case 192 Orders](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/192/orders)).
2. **ICJ Advisory Proceedings — *Legal Consequences arising from the Policies and Practices of Israel in the Occupied Palestinian Territory, including East Jerusalem* (Case 186, 19 July 2024):**
   - **Procedural Posture:** Advisory Opinion rendered under **Article 65 of the ICJ Statute** pursuant to UN General Assembly Resolution A/RES/77/247 ([ICJ Case 186 Advisory Opinion](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/186/advisory-opinions); [UN Summary PDF](https://www.un.org/unispal/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/186-20240719-sum-01-00-en.pdf)).
   - **Substantive Judicial Findings:** The Court determined that the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and the Gaza Strip constitute a single territorial unit under international law (paras. 78–102); that the 1993–1995 Oslo Accords cannot be understood to detract from Israel's obligations under the Fourth Geneva Convention (per Article 47 of GCIV); that Israel's settlement expansion, confiscation of land, exploitation of natural resources, forcible transfer of Palestinians, and discriminatory dual legal system breach the Fourth Geneva Convention, Article 3 of CERD, and the prohibition on acquiring territory by force; and that **Israel's continued presence in the Occupied Palestinian Territory is unlawful** (paras. 251–285), obligating Israel to bring its unlawful presence to an end "as rapidly as possible," cease all new settlement activities, evacuate all settlers, and make reparation, while obligating all States and the UN not to recognize the situation as legal or render aid or assistance in maintaining it.
3. **ICC Criminal Proceedings — *Situation in the State of Palestine* (`ICC-01/18`):**
   - **Procedural Posture:** Pre-Trial Arrest Warrants issued under **Article 58(1)(a) of the Rome Statute** on **21 November 2024** ([ICC Press Release 21 Nov 2024](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/situation-state-palestine-icc-pre-trial-chamber-i-rejects-state-israels-challenges); [Decision ICC-01/18-374 PDF](https://www.icc-cpi.int/sites/default/files/CourtRecords/0902ebd180a0ebd8.pdf); [OTP Statement 21 Nov 2024](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/statement-icc-prosecutor-karim-aa-khan-kc-issuance-arrest-warrants-situation-state-palestine)).
   - **Evidentiary & Jurisdictional Findings:** Pre-Trial Chamber I unanimously rejected Israel's pre-warrant jurisdictional challenge under Article 19(2) as premature prior to the issuance of a warrant (reaffirming the Court's 5 February 2021 territorial jurisdiction ruling covering Gaza and the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, while leaving post-warrant Article 19 challenges open, as reflected in subsequent appellate/pre-trial litigation in [Filing ICC-01/18 of 9 May 2025](https://www.icc-cpi.int/sites/default/files/CourtRecords/0902ebd180b7a918.pdf)). Applying the Article 58 threshold of **"reasonable grounds to believe"**, the Chamber issued warrants for:
     - **Benjamin Netanyahu** (Prime Minister of Israel) and **Yoav Gallant** (former Minister of Defense of Israel) as co-perpetrators for the war crime of starvation as a method of warfare and the crimes against humanity of murder, persecution, and other inhumane acts (Notice was taken that the Prosecution's material up to 20 May 2024 did not allow the Chamber to find all elements of the crime against humanity of extermination, though it found reasonable grounds for murder regarding deaths from malnutrition/dehydration, and superior responsibility for two incidents of intentionally directing attacks against civilians).
     - **Mohammed Diab Ibrahim Al-Masri ('Deif')** (Commander of the Al-Qassam Brigades) for crimes against humanity (murder, extermination, torture, rape and other forms of sexual violence) and war crimes (murder, cruel treatment, torture, taking hostages, outrages upon personal dignity, and sexual violence) committed from at least 7 October 2023 (proceedings regarding Ismail Haniyeh and Yahya Sinwar were terminated following confirmation of their deaths).
   - **Strict Procedural Caveat:** An Article 58 arrest warrant is an *ex parte* pre-trial measure to secure a suspect's appearance; it is **not** a confirmation of charges under Article 61 ("substantial grounds to believe") and **not** a criminal conviction under Article 66 ("beyond reasonable doubt"), and the presumption of innocence (Article 66(1)) applies until proven guilty at trial.

## 4. Competing Scholarly Interpretations (`interpretations`)

Historians and political scientists disagree fundamentally on why interim arrangements and civilian protection regimes between Israelis and Palestinians have repeatedly failed where interstate treaties (Egypt 1979, Jordan 1994) endured:

- **Structural Settler-Colonial & Asymmetric Power Paradigm (Rashid Khalidi, [*The Hundred Years' War on Palestine*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393175638_Geopolitical_Perspectives_on_Khalidi's_Hundred_Years'_War_on_Palestine), 2020):** Khalidi argues that the conflict cannot be understood as a symmetric dispute between two states, but rather as a century-long settler-colonial war backed by imperial patrons (Britain in 1917–1948; the United States thereafter). In this view, the 1993–1995 Oslo Accords were structurally designed to fail as a vehicle for liberation because they left Israel in control of borders, airspace, electromagnetic spectrum, water, and 60% of the West Bank (Area C), ignored the 1948 refugee right of return, and turned the Palestinian Authority into a security subcontractor policing an ongoing occupation while settler numbers tripled.
- **Archival Security-Realism & Mutual National Movement Tragedy (Benny Morris, *Righteous Victims*, 1999/2001; *1948*, 2008):** Drawing on Israeli and Western archives, Morris broke down traditional Zionist myths by documenting how Israeli military operations and expulsions interacted with Arab flight in 1948, yet his overarching interpretation views the conflict as a tragic, zero-sum collision between two national movements. In explaining the collapse of Oslo and the 2000 Camp David summit, Morris places primary weight on enduring Palestinian and Arab rejection of Jewish national sovereignty, Yasser Arafat's refusal to accept final-status compromises in 2000, and the strategic use of suicide terrorism by Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad, which convinced the Israeli public that there was "no partner" for territorial withdrawal.
- **Revisionist "Iron Wall" Critique of Israeli Maximalism (Avi Shlaim, [*The Iron Wall: Israel and the Arab World*](https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/153/), 2000/2014):** Shlaim examines Ze'ev Jabotinsky's 1923 "Iron Wall" doctrine, which posited that Arabs would only negotiate once convinced that Israel could not be defeated militarily. Shlaim argues that while the 1979 Egypt and 1994 Jordan treaties validated the second stage of Jabotinsky's theory—trading territory and mutual recognition for peace once military deterrence was established—Israeli right-wing leaders (especially Benjamin Netanyahu from 1996 onward) abandoned stage two on the Palestinian front, using military dominance and settlement expansion not as bargaining chips for a final settlement, but to perpetuate permanent domination.
- **Institutional Lock-In & "One-State Reality" Paradigm (Ian S. Lustick, [*Paradigm Lost: From Two-State Solution to One-State Reality*](https://online.ucpress.edu/jps/article/49/4/145/111934/Review-Paradigm-Lost-From-Two-State-Solution-to), 2019):** Lustick argues that decades of settlement expansion, legal integration, and infrastructural entanglement have rendered a negotiated two-state partition empirically impossible. Rather than viewing the current condition as a temporary occupation awaiting a better mediation formula, Lustick contends that a single state—Israel—already governs the entire territory between the Mediterranean Sea and the Jordan River through tiered legal regimes, meaning future durable arrangements will emerge not from diplomatic partition talks, but from long-term struggles over equal rights and citizenship within a single polity.
- **Comparative Mediation Design & Spoiler Management (Amr Hamzawy, Sarah Yerkes & Kathryn Selfe, [*U.S. Peace Mediation in the Middle East: Lessons for the Gaza Peace Plan*](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/02/gaza-trump-board-of-peace-negotiations-israel-lessons-from-oslo), Carnegie Endowment, Feb 2026):** Comparing four tracks of U.S. diplomacy (Egypt, Jordan, Syria, and Palestine), Hamzawy, Yerkes, and Selfe argue that mediation design and third-party enforcement strongly condition whether spoilers prevail. Where the U.S. combined sustained presidential leadership, clear final-status endgames, independent third-party monitoring (the MFO), conditional economic/military incentives, and public diplomacy (Egypt 1979, Jordan 1994), treaties held. Where final-status issues were deferred, interim steps lacked measurable accountability benchmarks for settlement expansion or violence, and domestic publics were left unprepared (Oslo 1993–2000, and risks identified in post-2025 Gaza governance plans under UNSC Resolution 2803), spoilers on both sides derailed implementation.
