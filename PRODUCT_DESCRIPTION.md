# OpenLine Department of History

## Ask what history says. Inspect the argument and the limit.

When a problem in the news feels new, it usually is not entirely new. Someone, somewhere, has faced something like it before: a disease spreading through a neighborhood, a factory fire that killed workers who had warned about it, banks failing in a panic, a shipping chokepoint under threat, a truce that held or fell apart.

The Department of History is a free, open-source tool that lets an ordinary citizen ask about those precedents in plain language and check the answer against the evidence.

## What you can do with it

**Ask a question the way you would say it.** "What happened when governments responded to banking panics?" "What can history tell us about a prolonged Strait of Hormuz disruption?" You do not need the jargon. The tool finds the relevant inquiry and shows you the evidence, or tells you plainly when your question is outside what it covers.

**See what people argued, and what actually happened.** For each precedent, the tool lays out the competing arguments of the time, what was tried, what followed, and where later historians disagreed with or refined what people believed then.

**Check where the comparison stops.** A past event is never a perfect match for today. The tool tests plausible-sounding analogies and shows the difference that breaks them: a physical limit, a different population, a different law. Knowing where the analogy stops is as useful as knowing where it holds.

**Inspect the evidence yourself.** Every claim names its source. You can open the source inspector, see what each source is and where it is thin, and withdraw a source to watch what happens: some claims are set aside, some narrow to the sources that remain, some are untouched. Five of the 44 sources are represented only by an abstract or synopsis, and the tool says so.

**Use it offline.** The whole thing is a single web page. Download it once and it works with no connection, no account, and no tracking. It makes zero external network requests.

## What it will not do

It will not tell you what will happen in 2030. It will not settle a political dispute. It will not tell you a historical claim is true just because it is written down. The hashes in the package prove the files have not been changed since they were curated; they do not prove the history is correct. The tool shows its limits on the page, next to its answers.

## The honest scale of it

Five inquiries. Forty-two sources. One hundred and four records. That is the whole library, and the tool never pretends otherwise. A question outside those five inquiries gets a clear refusal, not a confident guess.

## Who it is for

Anyone who wants to argue from history and wants to be able to show their work: citizens, teachers, students, journalists, and anyone who has ever been handed a historical analogy and wondered whether it actually holds up.

Free. Open source (MIT). Offline. No account.
